## The robotics problem

A mobile platform carrying a direction finder must locate and neutralize an unknown number of
radio-frequency emitters in a bounded region, and --- this is what separates the work from
conventional search-and-coverage --- must be able to **certify that none has been missed**. Three
properties make this a robotics problem rather than a detection problem. The number of emitters is
unknown, so the mission cannot stop on a counter and must stop on a *proof*. The absence of a
signal carries no distance information: an emitter that radiates into a half-plane of unknown
orientation can be invisible from a point a few metres away, so classical coverage reasoning
("the platform has been everywhere, therefore nothing is left") is unsound. And the guarantee must
survive a cluttered workspace, where the platform cannot travel in straight lines.

## Contributions

1. **A termination criterion that is decidable.** We derive a necessary and sufficient condition
   for a measurement network to detect an emitter of *arbitrary* orientation --- the emitter must
   lie in the convex hull of the network points within the worst-case reception radius --- and show
   that it forces the network beyond the region of interest. Every channel is then driven to one of
   two terminal states, neutralized or *certified absent*, so the mission ends with a proof rather
   than a timeout. An ablation shows that removing the certificate leaves the neutralized fraction
   unchanged but increases mission time by a factor of $4.8$.

2. **A measurement-network design formulated and solved as a routing problem.** The census network
   is a covering-salesman problem under the enclosure constraint; we give a rigorous point-count
   lower bound, a route-length bound, and a two-layer construction that uses $25$ points on a route
   $42\%$ shorter than the natural single-layer alternative.

3. **A clean separation of inference from planning.** Holding the evidence sets and the certificate
   fixed and changing one planning decision at a time, we show that the inference layer decides
   *whether* the mission succeeds (up to $-58\%$ when removed) while the planning layer decides
   *how fast* (up to $+78\%$ when degraded), and that the two axes are independent.

4. **An extension to obstructed workspaces**, where the enclosure condition becomes a
   line-of-sight condition: a disk-optimized network fails at $41.3\%$ of free-space positions and a
   greedy shadow-filling repair brings that to $2.3\%$, after which the census tour is re-planned on
   a visibility graph --- three of the disk-optimal points turn out to lie *inside* obstacles and
   must be relocated.

5. **A sensing model validated against an external standard rather than assumed.** No hardware was
   available for this study. Instead of postulating a bearing-error model we implement the complete
   physical layer --- specular multipath, uniform circular array, snapshot covariance, MUSIC --- and
   benchmark it against the **Cramér--Rao lower bound**, which it closely tracks to within $7\%$
   across the tested configurations. This establishes how accurately a compact direction finder
   *can* measure a bearing, and it shows that **antenna aperture, not integration time**, is what
   makes the guarantee hold.

6. **The limits of the guarantee, quantified**: a certificate-reliability map showing where the
   certificate can be trusted, and an honest statement of what remains open.

## Why it fits *Robotics and Autonomous Systems*

The paper is about a mobile robot completing a task with a guarantee, and about how to plan the
robot's motion so that the guarantee holds. Its three structural components are a task-level
termination criterion, a routing-and-covering formulation of the sensing plan, and motion planning
in a cluttered workspace. The sensing analysis is kept in service of those components: the array
study establishes the bearing uncertainty the planner must tolerate, and the reliability map lets
a reader decide whether the guarantee applies to their platform.

## Reproducibility

All code and data are openly available at https://github.com/ccx111145/b.t.measure and are
provided as supplementary material. Every number in the paper is regenerated by a single
self-check script (51 assertions, all passing).
